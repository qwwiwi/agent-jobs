# Benchmark rerun: Sonnet 5.5 high vs Opus 5.5 medium (2026-11-23)

Question: does the current routing rule hold? The rule: Sonnet at effort high is the default coder; Opus at effort medium is kept for large or sensitive work. This is a rerun of [the 2026-11-06 bench](2026-11-06-models.md) on the same nine agent-jobs issues and the same base commit, with fewer models and a newer reviewer.

## Summary

| | Sonnet 5.5 high | Opus 5.5 medium |
|---|---|---|
| Cost, all nine issues | $9.47 | at least $14.46 |
| Cost per issue | $1.05 | at least $1.61 |
| Sol findings, High | 12 | 12 |
| Sol findings, Medium | 11 | 12 |
| Sol findings, Low | 12 | 12 |
| Critical findings | 0 | 0 |
| `issue_fixed` yes / partly / no | 3 / 6 / 0 | 2 / 7 / 0 |
| Jobs auto-continued after a 60-minute timeout | 1 | 4 |

- **Quality was a tie.** Both models left the same number of High findings (12 each). Neither model fully fixed any of the three large issues. The yes/partly difference (3 against 2) comes from one issue, #155, where both reviews list the same six conditions as closed and differ only in the final label. Treat it as noise.
- **Sonnet was cheaper on 7 of 9 issues.** Opus was cheaper on #146 and #134. Opus total is a lower bound: one first attempt has no cost figure (see Cost accounting).
- **The cost gap is much smaller than on 11-06.** There it was $0.62 (Sonnet high) against $2.38 (Opus high) per task. Here it is $1.05 against at least $1.61. Opus at medium effort is cheaper than Opus at high was; Sonnet at high cost more this time. Treat that comparison with care: the reviewer changed and the 11-06 cost accounting of continued jobs is not stated.
- **Routing:** the data supports Sonnet high as the default coder. It does not show that Opus medium catches more on large or sensitive work. Details and limits are in [Conclusion for routing](#conclusion-for-routing).

## Method

- **Tasks:** agent-jobs issues #110, #145, #146 (small), #114, #154, #155 (medium), #119, #134, #135 (large). Sizes and what each issue asked for are the same as on 11-06: [task sizes](2026-11-06-models.md#what-the-task-sizes-mean).
- **Coders:** 18 jobs of kind `fix`, nine per model. Sonnet 5.5 at effort high and Opus 5.5 at effort medium. Each first attempt was limited to 200 turns, $50 and 60 minutes (a resumed attempt showed 100 turns, $25, 30 minutes). Every job worked on its own branch named `bench/<issue>-<model>-1123` and committed locally.
- **Review:** one `gpt-6.2-sol` high review job per branch. The reviewer reads the diff, cannot run the Go tests (its sandbox is read-only, no sockets), and ends with `BENCH-VERDICT: critical= high= medium= low= issue_fixed=yes|partly|no`. Findings come from reading and from small offline probes.
- **Brief:** the issue text plus fixed task rules, as on 11-06. The brief texts are not part of the evidence used for this report, so "identical for every model" is taken from the run plan, not re-checked.
- **Cost:** API-equivalent dollars from job usage. See Cost accounting for what is measured and what is estimated.

### Cost accounting

- The `usage` of a job covers only its last attempt. Five jobs timed out at 60 minutes and were auto-continued, so their first attempt is counted separately: Opus on #110, #145, #154, #155 and Sonnet on #134.
- **Cost** = last attempt + first attempt. **Turns** = both attempts. **Wall time** = 60 minutes + the duration of the last attempt for a continued job, otherwise the duration of the single attempt. Time spent waiting in the job queue before a start is not counted.
- First-attempt costs are **estimates at list price**, taken from the job-done lines, not from the usage record: #154 Opus $0.57, #134 Sonnet $0.93, #145 Opus $0.73, #155 Opus $0.80. They are marked with `†` below.
- First-attempt cost of **#110 Opus is unknown** (the done line has none; only 26 turns and 828,375 cache-read tokens). Its cost is shown as "at least $0.75". By the cache-read ratio of the other three Opus first attempts (about $0.35 to $0.40 per million tokens) it would be near $0.3, which would put the Opus total near $14.8. That is an extrapolation, not a measured figure.
- #155 Opus first attempt: the done line says 40 turns, the token table says 35. The report uses 40. With 35 the Opus total is 380 turns instead of 385. Cost is not affected.

## Quality comparison

Five concrete cases from the sol reviews and the coder reports. Quotes are verbatim from the reviews.

**1. #134, large: each model missed something the other one handled.**
Opus left the exit marker glued to unterminated output: "A child that prints `hi` without a newline produces `hiexit=0`." Sonnet's coder report says its marker "on its own line even when the command printed no trailing newline", and its review has no such finding. In the other direction, Opus's review says "The shell guard still rejects `gate run`", although the coder report says it "allowed it inside a job" (it changed only the CLI check). Sonnet's coder report says the opposite and leaves that decision to the owner; its review instead finds a stale-PGID risk: "signals a numeric PGID after reaping its leader. If the group is empty and the ID is reused before the signal, an unrelated group can receive SIGKILL." Both ended with 4 High findings.

**2. #154, medium: the same bug in both, a different turn-count mistake.**
Both patches select the replacement report by a heading prefix. Opus review: "Send a complete report, then `## Summary of timer\nTimer fired.` The second event replaces the delivered report." Sonnet review: "The prefix check selects the notification, the wrapper writes it over the report". The turn counts differ. Sonnet takes the larger value, so its fixture gives 87: "yields 87 instead of 88". Opus sums turns, but "Two complete reports with 87 and 1 turns select the revised text correctly but persist 1 turn instead of 88." Sonnet's coder report discloses the max-or-sum choice; Opus's report does not flag the two-report case as a risk.

**3. #146, small: the hardest "small" issue, for both.**
Both models fixed the direct cases and both missed the same three paths. Opus: "An absolute path to the same report skips its job’s repository check." Sonnet: "Queue a job with an allowed source, then deny its repository before launch. The wrapper loads the updated config but checks only the job’s own repository, so the already-copied attachment remains available to the runner." Sonnet had one more High: "An unreadable `.git` marker on a denied linked worktree leaves its source files eligible". Sonnet's report discloses that queued jobs are not re-checked; Opus's does not. This issue protects private repository content, and the size label "small" understated it.

**4. #119, large: a test scope that was too narrow.**
Opus ran the tests of four packages (`jobs`, `mcp`, `httpapi`, `store`) and reported all green. The review then finds a High in a fifth: "`cmd/agent-jobs/milestones_cli_test.go:50` — Both create and set fixtures expect bare keys to block tasks." It also finds a High design hole: "A normal task edit can erase a valid dependency". Sonnet ran the whole `go test ./...` through the gate and updated the old fixtures. Its High is of another kind: "shared-path guardrail is advisory". The planner can still leave out an edge for a shared money path. Sonnet's review also has a Medium for a reason-only edit that "can erase legacy dependencies", the same family as Opus's edit finding.

**5. #135, large: the same resolver gap, different severity labels, and an over-claim.**
Both patches leave Node's resolver able to fall back to `NODE_PATH`. Sonnet's report says the trusted mode "never looks in the cwd or `NODE_PATH`"; the review answers: "The resolver probe returned `scripts/ui-check.mjs` with `NODE_PATH` set and `MODULE_NOT_FOUND` with it cleared." Opus's report is more careful ("only absolute `NODE_PATH` entries"), and its review still finds that "`require.resolve(name, { paths })` adds Node’s global paths, including relative entries inherited through `NODE_PATH`." The same defect produced BLOCK for Sonnet (one blocking item) and SHIP WITH FIXES for Opus (none blocking). Read the verdict labels as loose; the bug is the same.

What the cases show together: the findings are mostly the same kind for both models (resolver and process edge cases, missing re-checks at launch time, incomplete fixture updates). Each model has a different blind spot per issue, and neither is consistently ahead.

## Caveats

- **One run per cell.** This is a signal, not statistics. A different reviewer run could move any single count by one or two, as the #155 pair shows (same closed list, different `issue_fixed`).
- **Static review.** Sol could not run the tests, so a verdict says what the reviewer found by reading and by small probes, not what a full test run would show. Some findings may be false; none was re-checked here.
- **Concurrency on a shared host.** Seven jobs ran at once on one machine, together with other work, and all heavy test runs went through one shared lock. Coder reports describe waiting for that lock (about 44 to 50 minutes where a figure is given, for #110 and #154 on Opus and #134 on Sonnet) and Go's default 10-minute test limit firing in `internal/jobs` under load. At least four of the five 60-minute timeouts were lock queueing (the fifth, #155 on Opus, has no such note), not model speed. Four of the five were Opus jobs; that should not be read as "Opus is slower". Wall time here is noisy and not a speed measure. It is shown for completeness only.
- **Auto-continue changes the numbers.** A continued job restarts from a saved session, so the first attempt's cost is partly estimated (see Cost accounting) and the 60 minutes in its wall time is the timeout, not the real working time.
- **Coder test evidence is thin.** Most coders could not get a clean full run of `internal/jobs` (default timeout under load, or lock waits), so they reported targeted tests and `go vet`. Most reported a red-then-green run for their new tests.
- **Reviewer changed since 11-06**, so absolute counts are not comparable between the two reports (see above). Opus effort also changed (high to medium), so this run cannot say how much of any difference is the effort level.
- **Subscription weighting** of Sonnet against Opus is not published. The report uses API-equivalent dollars.
- **Branches were not pushed and the raw reports are not stored in the repository** with this write-up. The numbers come from job usage records, the sol reviews and the coder reports of the run.

## Conclusion for routing

Current rule: Sonnet high is the default coder; Opus medium is used for large or sensitive work.

**For the default-coder half, the data supports the rule.**
- High findings: 12 against 12.
- Fully fixed: 3 against 2.
- Cost: Sonnet cheaper on 7 of 9 issues, $9.47 against at least $14.46 in total.
- On small and medium issues (six of nine), Sonnet had 7 High findings and Opus 6, at $4.64 against at least $8.60.

**For the large and sensitive half, this run neither supports nor contradicts the rule.**
- On the three large issues neither model fully fixed any: High findings 5 for Sonnet, 6 for Opus.
- The cost gap is smallest there ($4.83 against $5.86), so spending on Opus costs least on large work. That is a reason to keep it, but it is a cost argument, not a quality one.
- On the issue that guards private repository content (#146), Opus had one High finding fewer (3 against 4) and was cheaper. On the gate runner (#134) both had 4. Differences of one finding are inside the noise of a single static review.
- Opus medium was not compared with Opus high here, so the run cannot say whether the lower effort cost quality.

What the run does show regardless of model: 11 of 18 reviews ended in BLOCK, and none of the large or sensitive branches was clean. The review step and the full test gate on the exact head are what catch these defects, whoever writes the code. A more useful routing input than model choice is an issue-level risk flag: #146 was labelled small and behaved like a large, sensitive one.

To make the second half of the rule evidence-based, the next run needs Opus at high effort on the same large and sensitive issues, the same reviewer, and fewer jobs sharing the test lock.
